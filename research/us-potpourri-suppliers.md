## 6. Product reverse-engineering — likely bill of materials

Based on what commercially available U.S. decorative potpourri actually contains. Assumes a ~16–20 oz retail bag (Andaluca sells 20 oz; Hillhouse 13.5 oz — this is the market format).

### Composition by role

| Layer | % of volume (est.) | Typical materials | Cost character |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Base filler / bulk** | 55–70% | Wood excelsior & curls, bark chips, cedar pieces, dyed wood shavings, corn husk, buri/palm shavings, cheap dyed or bleached leaves, cellulose filler | **Cheapest per unit volume.** Drives cube and perceived fullness. |
| **Mid structure** | 15–25% | Small pinecones, rose hips, small pods, eucalyptus, statice, strawflower, cinnamon sticks | Moderate. Provides texture and color distribution. |
| **Hero / premium visual** | 5–15% | **Lotus pods, mahogany pods, bell cups, buddha nuts, wood roses**, large exotic pods, **dried orange/citrus slices**, preserved hydrangea heads, whole dried roses, protea | **Most expensive per piece.** Disproportionate share of perceived value — this is what sells the bag through a clear window. |
| **Fragrance system** | ~1–3% by weight | Fragrance oil + fixative (orris root powder, oak moss, or cellulose fixative granules) | Fragrance oil is often the **single largest line-item cost** after hero pods. |
| **Refresher vial** | — | Small vial of the same fragrance | Both Andaluca and Aromatique include one. Cheap, high perceived value, drives repeat purchase. **Copy this.** |
| **Packaging** | — | Clear PP/cello bag with hang hole, ribbon, printed hangtag, master carton | Modest, but custom print carries setup minimums. |

### Which inputs drive what

| Driver | Dominant contributors |
|---|---|
| **Visual appeal** | Hero pods, dried citrus slices, large flowers, color contrast against neutral filler |
| **Fragrance** | Fragrance oil load + fixative quality (fixative determines *longevity*, which is what reviews punish) |
| **Volume / cube** | Wood filler and bark — this is why potpourri is sold by the cup/gallon, not the pound |
| **Weight** | Pods, cones, wood, cinnamon |
| **Cost** | Fragrance oil, hero pods, and **freight** (see §7) |

**Design implication:** the cheapest lever on COGS is raising filler ratio; the cheapest lever on *perceived value* is concentrating hero pods against the bag's front face. These pull in opposite directions and the winner is determined by packaging orientation, not by blend ratio.

## 7. Landed cost and the economics of Option A vs. Option B

### The structural fact that dominates this category

**Potpourri is nearly all air.** It is one of the lowest-density products in home décor. This means:

- Freight is billed on **dimensional weight**, not actual weight
- A truckload "cubes out" long before it "weighs out"
- **Cross-country freight can rival or exceed the material cost of the goods themselves**

This single fact is why the Oxnard supplier outranks the Alabama supplier despite Alabama having the bigger, cheaper material base — and why you should not evaluate a quote until you have the **freight number alongside it.**

It also has a nasty implication for Option B: if you buy raw botanicals from Alabama or Ohio and blend in California, **you pay the high-cube freight on the inputs anyway**, and then again on outbound finished goods. Blending locally only wins if you source locally.

### Verified pricing anchors (weak — confirm all of these)

| Data point | Value | Source quality |
|---|---|---|
| Basic synthetic-scented blends, wholesale | **$3–$6 / lb** (2024) | ⚠️ Low — sourcing buying-guide, not a supplier quote |
| "Value sweet spot" for small business | **$6–$9 / lb** | ⚠️ Low — same source |

**I am deliberately not building a spreadsheet of invented per-bag costs.** With material, freight and labor all unquoted, a detailed cost model would look authoritative and be worthless. Instead, here is the decision framework and the specific numbers you need to collect to fill it.

### The comparison

| Dimension | **Option A — buy finished** | **Option B — buy raw, blend & pack** |
|---|---|---|
| **Material cost** | Higher per unit — you pay the blender's margin | Lower per unit, **if** you can buy hero pods at import volumes. At small volume you will pay *more* than a blender does. |
| **Freight** | One leg, but high-cube on finished goods | **Two legs of high-cube freight** unless sourced locally. Major hidden cost. |
| **MOQ** | Low. Faire-listed brands (Aromatique, Hillhouse, Park Hill) let you test with tiny commitments. Rachel's reportedly has no minimum. | High. Fragrance oil in 4–25 kg drums, botanicals in cases/pallets, custom-printed bags carry print minimums. You will commit to far more inventory. |
| **Labor** | Zero | Real but manageable. Hand-blending and bagging is slow, unskilled work. At California fully-loaded labor, throughput per person-hour is the variable to measure — run a timed pilot before modeling it. |
| **Working capital** | Low | High — you hold raw inventory across many SKU-lets (each pod type, each oil) instead of one finished SKU |
| **Speed to market** | Fast — weeks | Slow — sourcing, sampling, fragrance approval, packaging print runs |
| **Control / differentiation** | Low. You resell someone's blend, or get modest customization | High. Your blend, your fragrance, your look — genuinely defensible product |
| **Scalability** | Good, capped by supplier terms | Good, but scaling means capex and space |
| **Complexity / risk** | Low | Moderate–high. Fragrance IFRA/labeling compliance, flammability, botanical import/USDA phytosanitary rules on some materials, dust and fire safety in blending |
| **Gross margin potential** | Moderate | Higher **at volume**, worse at low volume |

### My recommendation

**Do not choose. Pursue the third option, which beats both.**

**Contract-blend to your specification** with a supplier who already buys botanicals at import scale — i.e. tell MGR (or Aromatique's new OEM line) *your* BOM, *your* fragrance, *your* bag and hangtag, and have them build it. You capture:

- Their material buying power on imported hero pods (which you cannot match under ~10,000 units)
- Their existing blending labor and equipment
- Zero capex, zero raw inventory, zero fragrance-compliance burden
- Full brand and design control

**Sequenced plan:**

1. **Now → 90 days.** Option A to learn the market. Buy finished from Aromatique and Hillhouse via Faire at minimal MOQ. Sell it. Learn which fragrances and colorways move, at what price, and what customers complain about. This costs almost nothing and de-risks everything after it.
2. **In parallel.** Open the MGR conversation immediately and ask for their private-label/OEM terms. This is the long-lead item.
3. **90–180 days.** Contract-blend your own SKU through MGR (first choice — local, low freight) or Aromatique's new plant (second — better scale, worse freight).
4. **Only if you exceed ~10,000 units/yr on a stable SKU** and want margin capture beyond that: bring blending in-house, sourcing base filler and cones from Columbia Pine Cones (California), bulk botanicals from LA Herb, and fragrance from HBNO (Chico) or American Oil Products (San Bernardino) — all in-state, which is the only configuration where Option B's freight math works.

**The economics answer to your question 12:** For Boring Deco today, **buying finished is better** — and it stays better until you're moving enough volume to buy hero pods by the container. The margin you'd "save" by self-assembly at low volume is smaller than the premium you'd pay for small-lot botanicals plus double freight. Assembling yourself becomes the right answer only at scale *and* only with California-local sourcing.
